Interview practice

All interview questions from the modules in one place. Answer each one out loud (or record yourself) before opening the outline. Tick a question when you can answer it confidently in under two minutes. Module 17 covers the interview process, STAR stories and case exercises.

questions I can answer

01 The IT Business Analyst role

What does a business analyst do?Strong answer
  • Bridge between business and IT: understand the need, describe the solution, check it was delivered.
  • Mention concrete activities: elicitation, process modeling, requirements and user stories, supporting testing and UAT, change management.
  • Give one short example (from your own experience, in BA terms).
  • Mention value: the right change, done right, saves money and risk.

Typical mistake: describing the BA as a note-taker or as a mini project manager without mentioning analysis.

You have no formal BA job title. Why should we hire you?Strong answer
  • Map your current work to BA activities: problem identification, proposal, agreement with IT, approval, testing.
  • Give one STAR story with a measurable or at least concrete result.
  • Show what you did to close gaps: this course, tools you tried (BPMN, SQL, Jira), a portfolio case study.
  • Domain knowledge: you understand insurance, approval processes and regulated environments.

Typical mistake: apologizing for lack of experience instead of translating the experience you have.

What is the difference between a business requirement and a functional requirement?Strong answer
  • Business requirement: a goal of the organization, for example shorter claim processing time.
  • Functional requirement: a specific behavior of the solution, for example extracting invoice data from an uploaded PDF.
  • Explain the chain: business → stakeholder → solution (functional and non-functional), plus transition requirements.
  • One example of each from the same project.

Typical mistake: giving two functional requirements and calling one of them "business" because it sounds important.

How do you handle a stakeholder who already has a solution in mind?Strong answer
  • Take the idea seriously and write it down.
  • Ask about the problem behind it: what happens today, what goes wrong, how often, what it costs.
  • Compare the proposed solution with alternatives against the need.
  • If the proposed solution is good, say so; if not, show the trade-off with facts and let the decision-maker decide.

Typical mistake: either building the requested solution without questions, or rejecting it to prove you know better.

02 How IT projects are delivered

What is the difference between waterfall and agile, and which do you prefer?Strong answer
  • Waterfall: sequential phases, scope fixed up front, changes expensive late; suits stable requirements and fixed-price contracts.
  • Agile: short iterations, frequent feedback, scope adapts; suits uncertainty and changing needs.
  • Most banks and insurers use a hybrid; give an example of what that looks like.
  • Preference: depends on context; explain what you would adapt as a BA in each.

Typical mistake: saying agile is always better or that agile means no documentation.

Describe the Scrum events and what a BA contributes to each.Strong answer
  • Sprint Planning: brings refined, ready backlog items, clarifies acceptance criteria.
  • Daily Scrum: answers blocking questions quickly, notes new clarifications.
  • Sprint Review: helps stakeholders see the increment against the need, captures feedback into the backlog.
  • Sprint Retrospective: raises issues such as unclear requirements or late business input.
  • Mention backlog refinement as ongoing work where the BA is central, and that it is not a formal event.

Typical mistake: listing the events correctly but saying nothing about the BA's contribution.

How would you work with an external vendor on a fixed-price project?Strong answer
  • Make requirements and acceptance criteria precise before the contract or work order.
  • Agree communication channels and a single point of contact for requirement questions.
  • Run a clear change request process; never agree extra scope informally.
  • Review vendor designs and test results against requirements; support UAT and sign-off.
  • Keep the relationship professional: firm on scope, cooperative on solving problems.

Typical mistake: treating the vendor as an enemy, or the opposite, agreeing to everything in meetings without recording it.

What happens between "developer finished" and "users have it in production"?Strong answer
  • Deployment to TEST, system and integration testing, defect fixing.
  • Deployment to UAT, business acceptance testing, sign-off.
  • Change approval (for example a change advisory board), release planning, rollback plan.
  • Release to PROD, release notes, user communication and training, monitoring after release.

Typical mistake: forgetting UAT and change approval, which are central in regulated organizations.

03 Problem analysis, process improvement and automation candidates

How would you find the root cause of a slow process?Strong answer
  • Clarify the symptom with numbers: which cases, how slow, since when.
  • Map the process (SIPOC, then AS-IS) and measure lead time vs working time.
  • Use fishbone to collect causes broadly, 5 Whys to go deep on the likely ones.
  • Verify with data (Pareto of delay reasons) and with the people doing the work.
  • Write a problem statement and agree it with the sponsor before proposing solutions.

Typical mistake: jumping straight to a tool or automation without showing where the time is lost.

How do you decide whether a process is a good candidate for automation?Strong answer
  • Criteria: rule-based, volume, stability, digital structured inputs, error impact.
  • First remove waste; do not automate a broken process.
  • Rough business case: hours saved × cost vs build and running cost; payback.
  • Consider ownership, maintenance, and IT approval of the tool.
  • Give a short example, such as intake of credit limit requests.

Typical mistake: saying "anything repetitive should be automated" without mentioning cost, stability or governance.

Walk me through a simple business case you could prepare.Strong answer
  • Baseline: volume, time per case, error rate, cost per hour.
  • Future state: expected time and errors after the change.
  • Costs: one-off and running.
  • Results: net annual benefit, payback period, multi-year view, freed capacity.
  • Non-financial benefits, risks and a sensitivity check; clear recommendation.

Typical mistake: presenting only the benefit and forgetting running costs or the assumptions behind the numbers.

You have twenty improvement ideas from a workshop and budget for three. What do you do?Strong answer
  • Agree criteria for impact and effort with the sponsor.
  • Get effort estimates from IT or the vendor.
  • Score and place ideas on an impact/effort matrix; mark mandatory and dependent items.
  • Present a recommendation with reasons; let the sponsor decide.
  • Keep the rest in a visible backlog rather than discarding them.

Typical mistake: prioritizing by who shouted loudest or by personal preference without visible criteria.

04 Process modeling with BPMN 2.0

What is BPMN and why would you use it instead of a simple flowchart?Strong answer
  • OMG standard (BPMN 2.0), shapes with defined meaning, readable by business and IT.
  • Has concepts a flowchart lacks: pools/lanes for participants, message flows, events such as timers and errors, boundary events.
  • Can be executed by a process engine; maps well to low-code flows.
  • Use a flowchart for quick sketches, BPMN when people must agree on a process or automation is planned.

Typical mistake: listing symbols without saying what problem the standard solves.

Walk me through how you would model and improve an approval process you do not know yet.Strong answer
  • Interview process owner and people doing the work, read existing documents, possibly observe.
  • Draw AS-IS quickly, play it back, correct it.
  • Mark pain points with impact (time, errors, volume).
  • Agree constraints and scope, draw TO-BE, write a gap list.
  • Turn gaps into requirements and validate TO-BE with stakeholders and IT.

Typical mistake: jumping straight to a TO-BE without validating the AS-IS with the people who do the work.

Explain the difference between exclusive, parallel and inclusive gateways with an example.Strong answer
  • Exclusive: exactly one path, for example "Limit above 1 M EUR?" yes or no.
  • Parallel: all paths at once, for example ARES lookup and country rating check simultaneously; joined by a parallel gateway that waits for both.
  • Inclusive: one or more paths depending on conditions, for example request the invoice copy if it is missing and/or the proof of delivery if it is missing; the inclusive join waits only for the paths that were started.
  • Mention that split and join types must match.

Typical mistake: saying parallel means "in any order" without explaining that the join waits for all paths.

How do you handle deadlines and exceptions in a BPMN model?Strong answer
  • Timer boundary events on activities: interrupting (cancel and reroute) or non-interrupting (escalate while work continues).
  • Error boundary events on service tasks for system failures, with a manual fallback path.
  • Event-based gateway when waiting for one of several outcomes (documents arrive or deadline passes).
  • Model the happy path first, then add exceptions agreed with stakeholders.

Typical mistake: modeling only the happy path and leaving exceptions as "will be handled manually".

How does a BPMN model help when working with a vendor or automation team?Strong answer
  • Shared picture of scope: which steps change, which systems are involved.
  • Task types show what is automated (service tasks) versus human (user tasks).
  • Each service task and boundary event points to an integration and its error handling.
  • Paths through the model become test scenarios for UAT.
  • Can be executed directly in an engine or translated into a low-code flow.

Typical mistake: treating the diagram as a deliverable by itself instead of linking it to requirements and tests.

05 Requirements elicitation

How do you elicit requirements from a department you have never worked with?Strong answer
  • Prepare: read documents, understand the domain vocabulary, identify stakeholders.
  • Start with interviews of key people, then observe the real work.
  • Draw the AS-IS and play it back.
  • Use a workshop to agree priorities or resolve conflicts, prototypes for screens.
  • Confirm in writing and keep track of sources.

Typical mistake: describing only "I would ask them what they need", without preparation, techniques or confirmation.

Two departments want opposite things. How do you handle it?Strong answer
  • Make the conflict explicit and document both positions with their reasons.
  • Go back to the business goal and the measurable objective.
  • Look for options that satisfy both (for example different paths by amount or risk).
  • If unresolved, escalate to the decision maker with the options and impacts, and record the decision.

Typical mistake: deciding yourself as the BA, or choosing the side of the more senior person without analysis.

What is the difference between a business requirement and a functional requirement? Give an example.Strong answer
  • Business requirement: why, organizational goal, for example "decide 80 % of requests within 3 working days".
  • Functional requirement: what the solution does, for example "the system shall retrieve buyer data from ARES by registration number".
  • Functional requirements must trace back to a business requirement.
  • Mention stakeholder, non-functional and transition requirements as further types.

Typical mistake: giving two functional requirements and calling one of them "business".

A stakeholder gives vague answers like "it should be user-friendly and fast". What do you do?Strong answer
  • Ask for concrete examples and numbers: how fast, compared to what, what is unacceptable.
  • Ask about a situation where the current system was too slow or confusing.
  • Turn the answer into a measurable non-functional requirement and confirm it.
  • Use a prototype to make "user-friendly" concrete.

Typical mistake: writing "the system shall be user-friendly" into the specification.

How do you use AI tools in requirements work?Strong answer
  • Draft question banks, role-play stakeholders, summarize, check ambiguity.
  • Only non-confidential or fictional data in public tools; otherwise tools approved by IT.
  • Always verify output and confirm results with real stakeholders.
  • Follow internal rules on labeling AI-generated content.

Typical mistake: describing pasting real meeting transcripts into a public chatbot.

06 Writing requirements and specifications

Here is a requirement: "The system should respond quickly and be user-friendly." How would you improve it?Strong answer
  • Point out it combines two topics and uses untestable words ("quickly", "user-friendly", "should").
  • Split into a performance NFR and a usability requirement.
  • Add numbers: response within X seconds for Y % of requests under Z load.
  • Make usability concrete: task completion time, error messages next to fields, test with users.
  • Mention you would confirm the numbers with stakeholders and IT rather than invent them.

Typical mistake: replacing one vague word with another ("efficient", "intuitive").

What would you put into a functional specification for an external vendor?Strong answer
  • Document control, context and goal, scope and explicit out of scope.
  • Actors, TO-BE process, numbered functional requirements with priority.
  • Business rules, UI/wireframes, data dictionary, integrations with error handling.
  • Measurable NFRs, acceptance criteria and UAT approach, open questions, glossary.
  • Mention review with the vendor and sign-off before development.

Typical mistake: describing only a list of features without scope, NFRs or acceptance criteria.

What makes a good requirement?Strong answer
  • Unambiguous, verifiable, atomic, consistent, feasible, necessary, traceable (ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148, IREB).
  • States what, not how, unless it is a real constraint.
  • Has an ID, source and priority.
  • Give one bad and one improved example.

Typical mistake: listing adjectives without showing how you check them in practice.

How do you handle changes to requirements after sign-off?Strong answer
  • Use change control: a change request with description, reason, requester.
  • Impact analysis using the traceability matrix: affected requirements, tests, cost, schedule (with the vendor).
  • Decision by the responsible person or change board; update the baseline, version and change log.
  • In agile, the change goes into the backlog, but contractual scope may still need a formal CR.

Typical mistake: quietly updating the document and telling the vendor in a hallway conversation.

What is a traceability matrix and why would you maintain one?Strong answer
  • A table linking each requirement to its source, business goal, design and test cases.
  • Shows untested requirements and requirements nobody asked for.
  • Supports impact analysis of changes and UAT coverage reporting.
  • Can be a spreadsheet or links in tools such as Jira and a test management tool.

Typical mistake: saying it is "for audit only" and not connecting it to testing and change impact.

07 Agile requirements: user stories, acceptance criteria and Jira

Write a user story with acceptance criteria for uploading documents to a claim.Strong answer
  • Specific role, capability and benefit: "As an exporter, I want to upload…, so that…".
  • 3–6 AC: allowed file types, size limit, document type, where the file is stored, behavior on error.
  • At least one Given/When/Then scenario for a negative case.
  • Mention open questions you would clarify (virus scanning, maximum number of files).

Typical mistake: writing only the story sentence with no acceptance criteria, or writing AC that describe UI layout instead of behavior.

What does INVEST mean, and which letter is most often violated in practice?Strong answer
  • Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable, each with a short explanation.
  • Most often violated: Small (stories too big) and Valuable (technical tasks written as stories); Independent is hard to achieve fully.
  • Show how you would fix it: splitting patterns, rewriting from the user's perspective.

Typical mistake: reciting the acronym without any example of applying it.

What is the difference between a product owner and a business analyst?Strong answer
  • PO: Scrum accountability, maximizes product value, orders the backlog, decides and accepts.
  • BA: analysis discipline (BABOK), elicitation, modeling, stories and AC, supports PO and team.
  • In practice roles overlap or are combined; in vendor projects they may sit on the client side.
  • You support the PO's decisions with analysis.

Typical mistake: saying the roles are the same, or that the BA decides priorities.

How would you split a story that is too large for one sprint?Strong answer
  • Split vertically, each slice delivers value.
  • Name patterns: workflow steps, business rules, data variations, happy/unhappy path, CRUD, defer performance.
  • Give a concrete example from a domain you know.
  • Check that the PO can prioritize slices independently.

Typical mistake: splitting by technical layers (front end, back end, database).

How do you use Jira and Confluence as a BA?Strong answer
  • Jira: epics and stories with AC, links to requirements and dependencies, backlog ordering with the PO, sprints, board.
  • JQL filters and dashboards for status reporting, for example open bugs by priority.
  • Confluence: specifications, process diagrams, decision log, meeting notes, glossary, linked to Jira issues.
  • Keep one source of truth and link instead of copying.

Typical mistake: only saying "I have used Jira" without describing what you did in it.

08 UML 2.5 for business analysts

Which UML diagrams do you use as a business analyst, and when?Strong answer
  • Use case diagram for scope, always backed by textual use cases.
  • Sequence diagram for integrations: order of calls, synchronous versus asynchronous, error branches.
  • Class diagram as a domain model to agree on business concepts and multiplicities.
  • State machine diagram for status lifecycles; testers derive test cases from it.
  • Activity diagram for logic inside a function; BPMN for cross-department processes.
  • A concrete example from your portfolio (e.g. the ARES lookup).

Typical mistake: listing all 14 diagram types from memory without saying when you would use any of them.

How would you describe an integration between two systems to a vendor?Strong answer
  • Start with the business purpose and trigger: what event starts the exchange.
  • A sequence diagram with participants, call order and every outcome (success, not found, invalid input, timeout).
  • A data mapping table: source field, target field, transformation, mandatory or optional.
  • Non-functional rules: timeouts, retries, volumes, security, logging.
  • Acceptance criteria and test scenarios for each branch.

Typical mistake: describing only the happy path and leaving error handling to the developer's imagination.

What is the difference between BPMN and UML?Strong answer
  • Both are OMG standards, but for different questions.
  • BPMN 2.0: business processes, who does what in which order, events and handovers between roles and organizations; can be executed by workflow engines.
  • UML 2.5: software systems, their structure (classes, components) and behavior (use cases, sequences, states).
  • In practice they are combined: BPMN for the business process, UML for the system parts.

Typical mistake: saying one is "better" without linking the choice to the question the diagram must answer.

Show me how you would model the lifecycle of an insurance claim.Strong answer
  • Name the states as they exist in the system (NOTIFIED, IN_REVIEW, APPROVED, REJECTED, PAID).
  • Draw a state machine diagram with triggers on each transition and guards where conditions apply.
  • Point out the questions it raised: re-opening rejected claims, buyer pays late, partial payments.
  • Explain how forbidden transitions become negative tests and validation rules for the developer.

Typical mistake: drawing a flowchart of the claims process instead of the states of one claim object.

09 Data and databases for analysts

Explain a relational database to someone who only knows Excel.Strong answer
  • Tables are like sheets, but each table describes one kind of thing and each column has a fixed type.
  • Rows are linked by keys instead of copying data: an invoice stores the buyer's ID, not the buyer's name and country.
  • The database enforces rules (mandatory fields, valid references), supports many users at once and keeps an audit trail.
  • You ask questions with SQL instead of filters and pivot tables.
  • A short example from the insurance domain.

Typical mistake: answering with database product names instead of explaining the idea.

How do you approach data quality in a project?Strong answer
  • Name the dimensions: completeness, validity, consistency, accuracy, uniqueness, timeliness.
  • Profile the data early with SQL queries: counts, nulls, duplicates, out-of-range values.
  • Quantify the impact and find the root cause, not only the bad rows.
  • Write preventive validation rules into requirements and test them.
  • Agree on ownership: who fixes data and who decides on edge cases.

Typical mistake: treating data quality as a one-off cleanup instead of fixing the cause.

What is a data dictionary and why would you write one?Strong answer
  • A table documenting each field: business name, meaning, type, format, mandatory flag, allowed values, source, owner, sensitivity.
  • It creates one shared meaning for business, vendor and testers.
  • It is the base for validation rules, interface mappings, migration mappings and test data.
  • Keep it versioned next to the specification and update it with change requests.

Typical mistake: copying column names from the database without writing what they mean in business terms.

What should a business analyst know about GDPR?Strong answer
  • What personal data is, and that it appears even in B2B systems (contact persons, sole traders, user accounts).
  • Principles that become requirements: purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation (retention), integrity and confidentiality (access control, security).
  • The BA flags and documents; legal or the DPO interprets the law and sets retention periods.
  • Practical rules: no production personal data in test without anonymization, no real data in public AI tools.

Typical mistake: saying "legal handles GDPR" and ignoring that the system design decides what data is collected and who sees it.

10 SQL for business analysts

What is the difference between INNER JOIN and LEFT JOIN? Give an example.Strong answer
  • INNER JOIN returns only rows that match in both tables.
  • LEFT JOIN returns all rows from the left table; columns from the right table are NULL where nothing matches.
  • Example: all buyers with their country; a buyer without a country disappears with INNER JOIN but stays with LEFT JOIN.
  • LEFT JOIN plus WHERE right.id IS NULL finds records without a related record (buyers with no credit limit).

Typical mistake: explaining joins with Venn diagrams only, without saying what happens to unmatched rows and why NULLs appear.

How have you used SQL in your work or projects?Strong answer
  • A concrete question you answered with numbers, e.g. how long credit limit decisions take.
  • Verifying test results: acceptance criteria rewritten as queries that must return zero rows.
  • Data quality checks: duplicates, missing values, contradictory dates, with counts and impact.
  • Sizing a problem for a business case.
  • What you would not do: change production data directly.

Typical mistake: saying "I know SQL" without a single example of a question it helped answer.

Write a query that shows the number of claims and total claimed amount per status.Strong answer
  • SELECT status, COUNT(*) AS claims, SUM(claimed_amount) AS claimed FROM claims GROUP BY status ORDER BY claims DESC;
  • Explain each clause briefly while writing.
  • Mention the currency issue: group by currency as well, or filter, before adding amounts.
  • Mention checking the result: total of counts equals SELECT COUNT(*) FROM claims.

Typical mistake: selecting a column that is neither in GROUP BY nor aggregated.

How do you check data quality with SQL?Strong answer
  • Completeness: IS NULL counts and COUNT(*) versus COUNT(column).
  • Validity: formats and ranges, e.g. length(ico) <> 8, due date before issue date.
  • Consistency: contradictions across fields and tables, e.g. PAID claims without payments (LEFT JOIN).
  • Uniqueness: GROUP BY ... HAVING COUNT(*) > 1.
  • Report count, examples, impact and root cause, and propose a preventive validation.

Typical mistake: listing problems without quantifying them or saying why they matter to the business.

11 APIs, JSON/XML and system integrations

Explain what an API is to a business stakeholder.Strong answer
  • A defined way for one system to ask another for data or to trigger an action, without a person in between.
  • Analogy: a service counter with a fixed form; you fill in the form (request) and get a standard answer (response).
  • A concrete example: PolicyHub asks the company register for a company's address instead of the underwriter copying it.
  • Benefits: fewer manual steps and typing errors, faster decisions, data always current.

Typical mistake: answering with technical terms (REST, JSON, OAuth) that the stakeholder did not ask about.

How would you specify an integration between the policy system and the claims system?Strong answer
  • Purpose, systems, owners, trigger and direction.
  • Choice of pattern (API, messaging, file) based on freshness, volume and reliability needs.
  • Data mapping table with types, mandatory flags and transformations.
  • Error handling: validation errors, timeouts, retries, idempotency, alerting.
  • Non-functional: volumes, response times, security, personal data, monitoring.
  • Test scenarios for every branch, agreed with the vendor and testers.

Typical mistake: specifying only the fields and the happy path.

What HTTP status codes do you know and what do they mean?Strong answer
  • Groups: 2xx success, 3xx redirection, 4xx client error, 5xx server error.
  • 200 OK, 201 Created, 204 No Content.
  • 400 invalid request, 401 not authenticated, 403 not allowed, 404 not found, 409 conflict, 429 too many requests.
  • 500 server error, 503 service unavailable.
  • Why it matters for a BA: each code needs a defined reaction in the requirements and a test.

Typical mistake: mixing up 401 (who are you?) and 403 (I know you, but you may not).

Have you ever worked with an API yourself?Strong answer
  • A concrete example: calling ARES for a company ID in Postman, reading the JSON, comparing 200, 400 and 404 responses.
  • Tests in Postman: status code and a field value.
  • Reading an OpenAPI file and finding gaps against requirements.
  • A data mapping derived from a real response, including transformations (IDs as text, address formatting).

Typical mistake: claiming API experience without being able to describe a single request and response.

12 Systems and architecture for analysts

What does it mean for you to design a feature "in line with the architecture"?Strong answer
  • Reuse existing systems, services and integrations before requesting new ones.
  • Respect the system of record for each data element.
  • Follow company standards (approved platforms, DMS for documents, API gateway for integration).
  • Include security, audit and other non-functional requirements from the start.
  • Involve the architect before the specification goes to the vendor.

Typical mistake: answering "I follow the architect's instructions" without showing you know what to check yourself.

Can you explain what happens technically when a user clicks "Submit" in a web form?Strong answer
  • Frontend validates input and sends an HTTP request with a JSON payload to the backend.
  • Backend authenticates the user and checks authorization.
  • Backend applies business rules and writes to the database in a transaction.
  • Backend may call other systems through APIs or send messages.
  • Backend returns a status and the frontend shows the result.

Typical mistake: getting lost in technical details instead of linking each step to the kind of requirement it needs.

How would you find out which systems are affected by a new requirement?Strong answer
  • Read existing architecture diagrams and system documentation.
  • Walk through the process and ask, for each step, which system holds the data.
  • Ask the architect and system owners; confirm data ownership.
  • Draw a simple context diagram and have it reviewed.

Typical mistake: assuming the system the users see is the only one involved.

What security and compliance aspects do you consider when writing requirements in a bank or insurer?Strong answer
  • Roles and least privilege; segregation of duties for approvals and payments.
  • Personal data and GDPR: purpose, scope, retention; involve the DPO.
  • Audit trail of changes and decisions.
  • Data location and vendor risk assessment for new suppliers (DORA for financial entities).
  • Environments: no production personal data in test without anonymization or approval.

Typical mistake: treating security as "IT's problem" instead of part of the requirements.

A business team wants to solve a problem quickly with an unapproved online tool. How do you respond?Strong answer
  • Acknowledge the business need and its urgency.
  • Explain the risks (data leaving the company, no support, audit).
  • Look for an approved alternative: existing system, approved low-code platform, or a fast-track request to IT.
  • If a temporary workaround is accepted, document owner, end date and replacement plan.

Typical mistake: either blocking the team without offering a path, or quietly letting shadow IT grow.

13 Testing, UAT and accepting vendor deliveries

What is the role of a BA in testing?Strong answer
  • Make requirements testable and review them (static testing).
  • Help testers understand business rules; review or write test scenarios.
  • Prepare and coordinate UAT with business users and test data.
  • Triage defects with the vendor; distinguish defect vs change request.
  • Report status and give a go/no-go recommendation.

Typical mistake: saying "testing is the testers' job" or describing only clicking through screens.

How do you derive test cases from a user story?Strong answer
  • Each acceptance criterion gives at least one positive test.
  • Add negative tests: invalid inputs, missing data, wrong role.
  • Use boundary values for ranges and decision tables for rule combinations.
  • Link each test to the requirement for traceability.

Typical mistake: only testing the happy path described in the story.

How would you organize UAT for a new system delivered by an external vendor?Strong answer
  • UAT plan: scope, entry and exit criteria, test data, participants, schedule, sign-off.
  • Business scenarios based on real cases, written with the business.
  • Kick-off and support for users, daily triage with the vendor, retests.
  • Status reporting and a recommendation based on exit criteria.

Typical mistake: starting UAT without agreed exit criteria or realistic test data.

What makes a good defect report?Strong answer
  • Clear title describing the symptom.
  • Environment, preconditions, numbered steps to reproduce.
  • Expected result with a reference to the requirement, and actual result.
  • Evidence (screenshot, record ID), severity and priority.
  • One defect per report, facts not opinions.

Typical mistake: "It doesn't work" with no steps or expected result.

The vendor wants the delivery accepted, but there are still open defects. What do you do?Strong answer
  • Check the open defects against the exit criteria and their business impact.
  • Separate blockers (critical/high) from items that can be fixed later.
  • Propose acceptance with conditions: a written list of defects and fix dates, or no-go if critical defects remain.
  • Let the business owner decide, with your recommendation.

Typical mistake: accepting under time pressure without documenting the open items.

14 Low-code automation and algorithmic thinking

Tell us about an automation you designed or built.Strong answer
  • Situation: the manual process, volume, pain points (time, errors).
  • Your role: analysis, logic design, agreement with IT, build, testing.
  • Design choices: trigger, systems, API vs RPA, error handling, approvals.
  • Governance: approved platform, owner, documentation.
  • Result: measured time saved, fewer errors, faster response.

Typical mistake: describing tool clicks without the business problem and measured result.

How would you decide whether a process should be automated with low-code or handed to IT?Strong answer
  • Criticality: what happens if it fails?
  • Volume and performance needs.
  • Systems involved: standard connectors vs write access to core systems.
  • Complexity of rules and expected change frequency.
  • Ownership and support available.

Typical mistake: treating low-code as the answer to every problem, or never considering it.

What is the difference between RPA and API integration, and when would you use RPA?Strong answer
  • RPA imitates user actions on the screen; API integration calls system interfaces directly.
  • API is faster, more stable, more secure and auditable.
  • RPA when no API exists (old desktop app), as a temporary bridge, with a plan to replace it.

Typical mistake: presenting RPA as the modern default.

How do you make sure an automation built by the business is safe in a regulated company?Strong answer
  • Use only approved platforms and environments; follow DLP policies.
  • Service account, least privilege, named owners.
  • Error handling and monitoring; documentation and support model.
  • Check personal data and where data is stored; involve IT and security early.

Typical mistake: "I built it myself in a free tool to save time."

15 AI in business processes

How would you approach a project to use AI in an internal process?Strong answer
  • Start with the problem and the process step, not the technology.
  • Measure the baseline; check data availability and permission to use it.
  • Define success metrics and build an evaluation set with business experts.
  • Design human-in-the-loop and fallback; write acceptance criteria with thresholds.
  • Address governance: approved tools, GDPR, AI Act classification with compliance, logging.
  • Pilot, measure, then scale; monitor after go-live.

Typical mistake: starting with "let's use ChatGPT for this" without defining what good looks like.

How do you use AI tools in your own work as a BA?Strong answer
  • Drafting stories, test cases, meeting summaries, first versions of documents.
  • Clear prompts with context, format, constraints and examples.
  • Always reviewing and correcting; removing invented content.
  • Only approved tools; no confidential data in public tools.

Typical mistake: claiming AI output is used as-is, or not mentioning data protection.

How would you test an AI feature that extracts data from documents?Strong answer
  • Representative evaluation set with expected outputs, including edge cases.
  • Metrics per field (accuracy), precision/recall for classification.
  • Thresholds agreed with the business; behavior when the model is unsure.
  • Re-test after model or prompt changes; monitor correction rates in production.

Typical mistake: testing with three nice examples and declaring it works.

What risks do you see in using AI at an insurer or bank?Strong answer
  • Confidential and personal data leaving the company; GDPR.
  • Hallucinations and errors in decisions with financial effect.
  • Bias and lack of explainability; audit requirements.
  • Regulatory classification under the AI Act; vendor risk for AI services.
  • Over-reliance by users; prompt injection.

Typical mistake: listing only "AI makes mistakes" without mitigations.

16 Stakeholders, communication, presenting and training

How do you handle two stakeholders with conflicting requirements?Strong answer
  • Document both requirements, their sources and reasons.
  • Link each to business goals and the problem statement; quantify impact where possible.
  • Look for a third option that meets both needs partly.
  • Escalate to the accountable decision-maker with a clear recommendation; record the decision.
  • Give a short example.

Typical mistake: deciding yourself which side is right, or simply picking the more senior person's view.

How do you keep stakeholders informed during a project?Strong answer
  • Identify and analyze stakeholders (power/interest grid).
  • Agree a communication plan: who, what, how often, channel.
  • Weekly status report with RAG, risks and decisions needed; honest amber/red.
  • Demos at sprint reviews; involving users in testing.
  • Adjust format to the person (report vs short call).

Typical mistake: describing only "I send emails" without structure or tailoring.

Tell me about a time you had to present something to people with a different background.Strong answer
  • Situation and audience: who, what they knew, what they needed.
  • Structure: problem → options → recommendation → ask.
  • What you adapted: language, level of detail, visuals.
  • Result: decision taken, feedback received.
  • What you would do differently.

Typical mistake: talking about the content of the presentation instead of how you adapted it to the audience.

How would you say no to a senior manager's request that is out of scope?Strong answer
  • Acknowledge the need behind the request.
  • Explain the constraint factually: scope, budget, timeline, contract.
  • Offer options: change request with impact, swap with a lower-priority item, later release.
  • Let the right person decide; confirm in writing.

Typical mistake: either agreeing to avoid conflict, or refusing without offering any path forward.

17 Interview preparation and portfolio

Tell me about yourself.Strong answer
  • Current role in insurance in one sentence.
  • BA-type work you already do: process improvement, automation, agreeing with IT, testing.
  • Systematic preparation and portfolio.
  • Why this role, linked to two points from the ad.
  • 60–90 seconds.

Typical mistake: a chronological life story or a recital of the CV.

Why do you want to become a business analyst, and why should we hire someone without a BA title?Strong answer
  • You already do analysis in practice; give one concrete example.
  • Domain knowledge of insurance and approval processes.
  • Proof of skills: portfolio with BPMN, stories, SQL, API, UAT, automation.
  • Motivation to learn, shown by the training you completed.

Typical mistake: apologizing for the career change instead of presenting it as an advantage.

Tell us about a process you improved.Strong answer
  • STAR: situation with volume and pain, your task.
  • Actions: mapping, measuring, rules, design agreed with IT, approval, testing.
  • Result with numbers and what you learned.

Typical mistake: talking only about the tool, not the analysis and the result.

How do you gather requirements from stakeholders who disagree?Strong answer
  • Understand each stakeholder's goal and constraints separately.
  • Bring facts: data, process maps, costs of options.
  • Separate needs from proposed solutions; look for a common goal.
  • Escalate to the decision maker with clear options if needed; document the decision.

Typical mistake: "I do what the most senior person says."

Here is a business problem: claims take too long to process. How would you approach it?Strong answer
  • Clarify goal, volume, constraints.
  • Stakeholders, AS-IS sketch, pain points with data.
  • TO-BE options, key requirements, risks, next steps (baseline, MVP).
  • Think aloud and check in with the interviewer.

Typical mistake: proposing a tool (for example "use AI") in the first minute.

Write a SQL query: list buyers with the total amount of unpaid invoices, highest first.Strong answer
  • Explain the tables and join first (buyers, invoices).
  • Use JOIN, a WHERE filter for unpaid invoices (for example paid_date IS NULL), GROUP BY buyer, SUM, ORDER BY DESC.
  • Mention LEFT JOIN if buyers without unpaid invoices should also appear.
  • Point out that amounts in different currencies cannot simply be added: group by currency as well, or convert first.
  • Say how you would verify the result (count rows, check one buyer manually).

Typical mistake: freezing on syntax instead of explaining the logic step by step.

How would you explain what an API is to a business colleague?Strong answer
  • An agreed way for one system to ask another for data or an action.
  • Analogy: ordering from a menu at a restaurant; the kitchen is hidden.
  • Example: checking a buyer in the company registry by company ID, getting JSON back.
  • Why it matters: faster, more reliable than retyping or screen automation.

Typical mistake: a technical definition the colleague would not understand.

How do you work with an external vendor on a delivery?Strong answer
  • Clear specification and acceptance criteria as the shared basis.
  • Regular check-ins, questions log, documented decisions.
  • Testing and UAT, defect triage, defect vs change request.
  • Status reporting and escalation when needed; respectful but firm.

Typical mistake: treating the vendor as an opponent, or accepting deliveries without checking them.
