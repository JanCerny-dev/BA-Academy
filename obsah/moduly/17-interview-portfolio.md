## Why this matters
Knowing the topics is half of the job; the other half is showing it in a CV, a portfolio and an interview. A career changer competes with candidates who already have "Business Analyst" in their job title. Your advantage is real experience with business processes, approvals, automation and testing in an insurer, plus a portfolio that proves you can use the tools. This module turns what you learned into stories, documents and answers.

## Quick version
**What employers look for in a junior or career-change BA** (based on typical job ads like the target one):
- Logical, structured thinking and clear communication.
- Domain knowledge: insurance and finance experience counts.
- Basic technical literacy: SQL, API, JSON/XML, BPMN or UML.
- Ability to work with stakeholders and vendors.
- Evidence: examples from your work and a portfolio.

**Your career-change story.** Present it as a continuation, not a jump: "In my current role in insurance I noticed inefficient processes, proposed automations, agreed them with IT, got them approved and tested them. That is business analysis work. I now want to do it full-time and have trained systematically in modeling, requirements, SQL, APIs and testing." Back it with **STAR** examples: Situation, Task, Action, Result.

**CV and LinkedIn:**
- Use the words from the job ad: requirements, specification, stakeholders, BPMN, SQL, API, UAT, low-code, AI.
- Describe your current job in BA language with numbers: "Designed and tested an approval automation that cut processing time from 2 days to 4 hours."
- Add a "Projects" section with the capstone and a link to the portfolio.

**Portfolio** (from the capstone): BPMN AS-IS and TO-BE, a short specification, user stories with acceptance criteria, SQL queries with results, a Postman collection, an automation (screenshots + one-pager), a UAT plan and test cases. Fictional company, real skills.

**Interview formats:**
- HR screen (motivation, availability, salary expectations).
- Competency interview (behavioral questions, STAR).
- Case study or whiteboard exercise.
- Take-home task (write stories, model a process, analyze a request).
- Technical questions (SQL, API, JSON, BPMN).

**Whiteboard case method:** clarify the goal → identify stakeholders → sketch AS-IS → find pain points → propose TO-BE → list key requirements → name risks and next steps. Think aloud.

**Questions to ask the employer:** who are the stakeholders, how requirements are documented, which vendors and tools are used, how success is measured in the first months.

**Salary.** Research current ranges for junior IT Business Analyst roles in Prague on job portals and salary surveys close to your interview date, and give a range, not a single number.

**First 90 days:** learn the domain and systems, meet stakeholders, deliver something small and useful, document what you learn.

**How to prepare in practice:**
- Write 5–7 STAR stories and say each aloud in under two minutes.
- Practice two or three whiteboard cases with a timer.
- Write a JOIN with GROUP BY on paper and explain a JSON response without notes.
- Prepare a 5-minute walkthrough of your portfolio.
- Record yourself and fix the weakest answers.

**Common mistakes:** answering with "we" instead of "I", jumping to a solution before clarifying the problem, apologizing for the career change, claiming skills you cannot show, and sharing confidential details from your current employer. Interviewers notice all of them.

## Deep dive

### What Czech employers look for in junior BAs [P1]
Job ads for IT Business Analyst roles in Prague, like the target ad for this course, typically combine four groups of requirements:

| Group | Typical wording in ads | How you show it |
|---|---|---|
| Analytical thinking | logical thinking, structured approach, attention to detail | Case study, STAR stories, clean documents |
| Communication | working with business and IT, presenting, training colleagues | How you explain in the interview; examples of agreements with IT |
| Methods | BPMN or UML, user stories, specifications, testing | Portfolio documents |
| Technical basics | SQL, API, JSON/XML, low-code, AI | Portfolio, short technical questions, live SQL |

Other things that often matter: English (many teams and vendors work in English, and interviews may switch languages), domain knowledge in finance or insurance, and willingness to learn the company's systems. For a career changer, employers mostly want to see that you already think like an analyst and have done the basics yourself.

### Telling the career-change story [P1]
Structure (60–90 seconds):
1. **Where you are:** your role in insurance, in one sentence, without internal details or names.
2. **The bridge:** what BA-type work you already do: noticing process problems, proposing automation, agreeing it with IT, getting approval, testing.
3. **What you did to prepare:** systematic training (process modeling, requirements, SQL, APIs, testing, low-code, AI) and a portfolio project.
4. **Why this role:** link to the ad: specifications for vendors, automation, AI integration.

Example: "I work in export credit insurance, close to underwriting and claims. Over the last years I kept finding manual steps, such as copying data between e-mail, Excel and our policy system. I proposed automations, agreed the design with IT, got them approved and tested them before go-live. I realized that this is business analysis, so I trained systematically: BPMN, user stories, SQL, APIs, UAT and low-code, and I built a portfolio case study. Your role combines vendor specifications, automation and AI, which is exactly the direction I want to grow in."

**STAR stories.** Prepare 5–7 stories that you can adapt to many questions.

| STAR | Content | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Context in 1–2 sentences | Enough for the listener to understand; no internal names |
| Task | Your responsibility | Make your role clear |
| Action | What *you* did, step by step | Use "I", not "we"; this is the longest part |
| Result | Outcome with numbers, plus what you learned | Hours saved, errors reduced, approval obtained |

Example STAR (process automation in an insurer, numbers are illustrative; use your own):
- **S:** Credit limit requests came by e-mail; an assistant retyped them into a spreadsheet and checked each buyer manually on the company registry website. About 40 requests a week.
- **T:** I wanted to reduce manual work and errors without changing the core system.
- **A:** I mapped the process, measured the time per request on a sample, wrote down the rules, proposed an automation using the company's approved platform, discussed the design with IT (connectors, accounts, error handling), got approval from the team lead, and tested it with real-looking cases including errors.
- **R:** Processing time per request dropped from about 15 to 4 minutes, copy errors disappeared, and the flow has a documented owner. I learned to agree the technical design with IT before building.

Story topics to prepare: an automation you proposed; a conflict between stakeholders; a mistake you found in testing; a time you had to explain something complex simply; a time you had to say no or push back; a time you worked with an external party (vendor, IT, auditor).

### CV and LinkedIn for BA roles [P1]
**CV structure (1–2 pages):**
1. Headline: "Business Analyst (career change from insurance operations)" or similar.
2. Summary (3–4 lines): domain, BA skills, tools, what you are looking for.
3. Skills: grouped (Analysis: BPMN, user stories, requirements, UAT · Technical: SQL, REST API, JSON/XML, Postman · Tools: Jira, Confluence, Power Automate/Make · AI: prompt design, evaluation).
4. Experience: each bullet as action + result, in BA vocabulary.
5. Projects: the capstone, with a portfolio link.
6. Education and certifications (planned certifications can be listed as "in preparation").

Rewrite weak bullets:

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Responsible for processing requests | Analyzed the request handling process and proposed an automation that reduced manual work by ~70% (≈350 hours/year) |
| Helped with testing | Prepared test scenarios and tested a new approval flow with business users before go-live; reported 12 defects to IT |
| Worked with IT | Agreed requirements and design of 3 automations with IT, including access rights and error handling |

Rules: numbers you can defend in an interview, no confidential details, no internal system names that mean nothing outside your company.

**LinkedIn:** headline with target role and keywords; "About" section with the career-change story; featured section with the portfolio; skills matching the ad. Recruiters search by keywords, so "Business Analyst", "BPMN", "SQL", "UAT", "Power Automate" should appear in text.

### Your portfolio [P1]
Built from the capstone case study (ExportShield). Each item: one short paragraph of context, the artifact, and what it shows.

| Item | What it shows | Format |
|---|---|---|
| BPMN AS-IS and TO-BE | Process modeling, finding pain points | PNG/PDF from bpmn.io or Camunda Modeler |
| Short specification | Writing for a vendor | PDF, 3–5 pages |
| User stories with acceptance criteria | Agile requirements, Gherkin | Table or Jira export |
| SQL queries with results | Data skills | Query + result screenshot |
| Postman collection | API understanding | Exported collection + screenshots |
| Automation | Low-code, algorithmic thinking | Pseudocode, flow screenshots, one-pager |
| UAT plan + test cases | Testing, quality | One-pager + test case table |
| AI evaluation (optional) | AI in processes, critical thinking | Evaluation table + conclusion |

Format: a single PDF (10–20 pages) or a simple web page, plus the PDF for sending. Start with a one-page overview: the case, your role (all of it, it's your project), the list of artifacts. State clearly that ExportShield is fictional. Never include real company documents.

| Format | What they test | How to prepare |

| Competency interview | Past behavior | 5–7 STAR stories |

| Take-home task | Quality of documents | Templates from this course; time-box; state assumptions |

### Running a whiteboard case [P1]
Example prompt: "Our claims department receives claim notifications by e-mail and processing takes too long. How would you approach it?"

1. **Clarify (2–3 min).** What does "too long" mean? Current and target time? Volume? Who complains? Any constraints (budget, systems, deadline)?
2. **Stakeholders.** Exporters, claims handlers, claims manager, finance (payments), IT, vendor, compliance.
3. **AS-IS.** Sketch the process in 5–8 boxes: e-mail → registration → document check → request missing docs → assessment → approval → payment.
4. **Pain points.** Where is time lost? Waiting for documents, manual retyping, unclear ownership, approvals queue.
5. **TO-BE.** Web form with mandatory fields, automatic registration in ClaimDesk, document checklist, automatic reminders, AI-assisted document sorting with human check, approval routing by amount.
6. **Requirements.** 4–6 key ones, including one non-functional (audit trail) and one data-related.
7. **Risks and next steps.** Data quality, vendor capacity, GDPR, change management. Next: measure baseline, confirm with stakeholders, MVP scope.

Think aloud, write as you go and check in with the interviewer ("Should I go deeper on the TO-BE or the requirements?"). The structure matters more than a perfect solution.

### Questions to ask the employer [P2]
- Who are the main stakeholders of this role, and how is work with vendors organized?
- How are requirements documented today (tools, templates, level of detail)?
- What does a typical project look like, from request to go-live?
- Which low-code and AI tools are approved, and who governs them?
- What would a successful first three months look like?
- How is the BA team organized, and who would I learn from?
- What is the biggest challenge for the team this year?

### Salary expectations [P2]
Salary ranges change quickly and differ by company type (bank, insurer, consultancy, vendor). Before an interview, check several current sources: ranges in job ads for similar roles in Prague, salary surveys published by recruitment agencies, and conversations with people in the field. Then:
- give a range, with your target slightly above the middle,
- say whether it is gross monthly,
- consider the whole package (bonus, home office, training budget, meal vouchers),
- as a career changer, be realistic: you may enter at junior level and grow quickly with demonstrated results.

### Your first 90 days [P2]
| Period | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–30 | Learn domain, systems, people; read existing documentation | Stakeholder map, glossary, notes on main processes |
| Days 31–60 | Take over a small piece of work; join testing or UAT | First specification or stories, defect reports |
| Days 61–90 | Deliver end to end; suggest one improvement | A delivered change, an improvement proposal with data |

Habits: ask questions early, write things down, confirm agreements in writing, and keep a log of your results for the next review.

### Question bank: the remaining 17 questions [P1]
The 8 most important questions are in the Interview corner below. Here are 17 more, with short hints.

**Motivation**
1. *Why do you want to move from your current role into business analysis?* – Continuation of what you already do; link to the ad.
2. *What do you know about our company and this role?* – Research the company, mention one specific link to your experience.
3. *Where do you see yourself in three years?* – Growing as a BA in a domain; possibly specializing (process, data, product).

**Behavioral**
4. *Tell us about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder.* – STAR; focus on understanding their need and finding data.
5. *Describe a mistake you made and what you learned.* – A real, small mistake; show what you changed.
6. *Tell us about a time you had to explain something complex to a non-technical person.* – Analogy, visual, checking understanding.
7. *How do you handle changing requirements late in a project?* – Impact analysis, change request process, transparent communication.
8. *Describe a situation when you worked under time pressure.* – Prioritization, communication, what you left out and why.

**Technical**
9. *What is the difference between INNER JOIN and LEFT JOIN?* – LEFT keeps all rows of the left table; use example customers without claims.
10. *What is a REST API? What are GET and POST?* – Interface over HTTP; GET reads, POST creates; mention status codes.
11. *Read this JSON and tell me what it describes.* – Objects, arrays, keys and values; walk through calmly.
12. *What is the difference between a functional and a non-functional requirement?* – What vs how well; give one testable NFR.
13. *What is an exclusive gateway in BPMN?* – One path chosen based on a condition; example amount above limit.
14. *How do you write acceptance criteria?* – Testable, Given/When/Then, include negative cases.
15. *What is the difference between severity and priority of a defect?* – Impact vs urgency, with an example where they differ.

**Case**
16. *Our finance team wants a new report. How do you start?* – Who uses it, what decisions, data sources, frequency, existing reports.
17. *A vendor delivered late and with defects. What do you do?* – Facts, triage, impact, escalation path, lessons learned.

## Key terms
- **STAR** — a structure for behavioral answers: Situation, Task, Action, Result.
- **Competency interview** — an interview that asks about past behavior to predict future performance.
- **Case study interview** — a live exercise where you analyze a business problem in a structured way.
- **Take-home task** — an assignment you complete on your own before or between interview rounds.
- **Portfolio** — a collection of work samples that proves your skills.
- **Capstone** — the final case study project of this course, the source of your portfolio.
- **Career-change story** — a short, structured explanation of why and how you move into a new profession.
- **Keyword matching** — using the same terms as the job ad so recruiters and search tools find your CV.
- **Elevator pitch** — a 30–60 second summary of who you are and what you offer.
- **Stakeholder map** — an overview of people involved, their interest and influence.
- **Gross salary** — salary before tax and social and health insurance deductions.
- **Onboarding** — the first period in a new job when you learn the organization, people and systems.

## Hands-on
:::exercise id="m17-e1" title="Write 5 STAR stories" min="90" tool="Text editor"
**Goal:** prepare stories you can use in any competency interview.

1. Pick 5 topics: an automation you proposed; a stakeholder conflict; a problem you found by testing; explaining something complex; working with IT or an external party.
2. For each, write S and T in 2–3 sentences, A as 4–6 bullet points with "I", R with at least one number.
3. Remove internal names, client names and confidential details. Use generic descriptions ("our policy system", "a large client").
4. Read each aloud and time it: target 90–120 seconds.
5. Map each story to at least two interview questions from this module.

**Done when:** you have 5 stories, each under 2 minutes, each with a measurable result and no confidential details.
:::

:::exercise id="m17-e2" title="45-minute mock case study" min="45" tool="Paper or Miro"
**Goal:** practice a whiteboard case under time pressure.

Prompt: *"ExportShield's credit limit decisions take on average 6 business days. Exporters complain and some move to competitors. The head of underwriting asks you to propose how to cut this to 2 days. You have 45 minutes: 30 to prepare, 15 to present."*

1. Set a timer. Use paper or [Miro](https://miro.com/).
2. Follow the case method: clarify (write the questions you would ask and assume answers), stakeholders, AS-IS, pain points, TO-BE, 5 key requirements, risks, next steps.
3. Present to a friend or record yourself for 15 minutes.
4. Afterwards, compare with the credit limit material in modules 04, 14 and 15 and note what you missed.

**Done when:** you have a one-page sketch, a recording or feedback, and a list of three things to improve.
:::

:::exercise id="m17-e3" title="Record yourself answering 5 questions" min="45" tool="Phone camera"
**Goal:** hear and see how you come across.

1. Choose 5 questions: "Tell me about yourself", one behavioral, one technical (SQL or API), one case question, "Why this role?".
2. Record each answer on your phone without notes.
3. Watch it back. Check: length (under 2 minutes), structure, filler words, concrete examples, eye contact.
4. Re-record the two weakest answers.

**Done when:** you have recorded each question at least once and the two weakest at least twice, with notes on what improved.
:::

:::exercise id="m17-e4" title="Build the portfolio" min="240" tool="Word/Google Docs or a simple web page"
**Goal:** assemble your capstone work into a portfolio you can send.

1. Create a cover page: title, one-paragraph case description (ExportShield is fictional), your role, list of artifacts.
2. For each artifact (BPMN, specification, stories, SQL, Postman collection, automation, UAT plan): a short context paragraph, the artifact or a screenshot, and "what this shows".
3. Keep it to 10–20 pages. Export as PDF.
4. Optional: publish a simple page with the same structure and link it from your CV and LinkedIn.
5. Ask one person to review it for clarity and typos.

**Done when:** the PDF is complete, contains no real company data and a reviewer understood what you did in under 10 minutes.
:::

## Self-check
:::q
Why should a career changer present the move as a continuation rather than a new start?
---
Employers hire for evidence of ability. Describing existing work (process improvement, automation, agreement with IT, testing) in BA terms shows you already do parts of the job. It turns "no BA title" into "BA experience in a different role", backed by examples and a portfolio.
:::

:::q
What makes the Action part of a STAR answer strong?
---
It is specific, in the first person and step by step: what you analyzed, whom you involved, what you decided and why. It is the longest part of the answer. Vague "we handled it" statements hide your contribution.
:::

:::q
Why use keywords from the job ad in your CV?
---
Recruiters and applicant tracking systems search for those terms, and the hiring manager quickly checks for them. Using the same words (requirements, BPMN, SQL, API, UAT, low-code) shows the match directly, as long as you can back each one with an example.
:::

:::q
What are the steps of a whiteboard case, and why start with clarification?
---
Clarify, stakeholders, AS-IS, pain points, TO-BE, requirements, risks and next steps. Clarifying first shows that you don't jump to solutions, and it gives you facts (volume, constraints, goal) that make the rest of the answer relevant.
:::

:::q
What should a portfolio based on a fictional case make clear?
---
That the company and data are fictional, what your role was, and what each artifact demonstrates. It should contain no real employer or client data. The value is in showing the methods and tools, not in the company.
:::

:::q
How do you handle the salary question in an HR screen?
---
Research current ranges for similar roles in Prague before the interview, give a range rather than a single number, state that it is gross monthly, and consider the whole package. Be realistic about entering at junior level while pointing to your domain experience.
:::

## Interview corner
:::interview
Tell me about yourself.
---
- Current role in insurance in one sentence.
- BA-type work you already do: process improvement, automation, agreeing with IT, testing.
- Systematic preparation and portfolio.
- Why this role, linked to two points from the ad.
- 60–90 seconds.

Typical mistake: a chronological life story or a recital of the CV.
:::

:::interview
Why do you want to become a business analyst, and why should we hire someone without a BA title?
---
- You already do analysis in practice; give one concrete example.
- Domain knowledge of insurance and approval processes.
- Proof of skills: portfolio with BPMN, stories, SQL, API, UAT, automation.
- Motivation to learn, shown by the training you completed.

Typical mistake: apologizing for the career change instead of presenting it as an advantage.
:::

:::interview
Tell us about a process you improved.
---
- STAR: situation with volume and pain, your task.
- Actions: mapping, measuring, rules, design agreed with IT, approval, testing.
- Result with numbers and what you learned.

Typical mistake: talking only about the tool, not the analysis and the result.
:::

:::interview
How do you gather requirements from stakeholders who disagree?
---
- Understand each stakeholder's goal and constraints separately.
- Bring facts: data, process maps, costs of options.
- Separate needs from proposed solutions; look for a common goal.
- Escalate to the decision maker with clear options if needed; document the decision.

Typical mistake: "I do what the most senior person says."
:::

:::interview
Here is a business problem: claims take too long to process. How would you approach it?
---
- Clarify goal, volume, constraints.
- Stakeholders, AS-IS sketch, pain points with data.
- TO-BE options, key requirements, risks, next steps (baseline, MVP).
- Think aloud and check in with the interviewer.

Typical mistake: proposing a tool (for example "use AI") in the first minute.
:::

:::interview
Write a SQL query: list buyers with the total amount of unpaid invoices, highest first.
---
- Explain the tables and join first (buyers, invoices).
- Use JOIN, a WHERE filter for unpaid invoices (for example `paid_date IS NULL`), GROUP BY buyer, SUM, ORDER BY DESC.
- Mention LEFT JOIN if buyers without unpaid invoices should also appear.
- Point out that amounts in different currencies cannot simply be added: group by currency as well, or convert first.
- Say how you would verify the result (count rows, check one buyer manually).

Typical mistake: freezing on syntax instead of explaining the logic step by step.
:::

:::interview
How would you explain what an API is to a business colleague?
---
- An agreed way for one system to ask another for data or an action.
- Analogy: ordering from a menu at a restaurant; the kitchen is hidden.
- Example: checking a buyer in the company registry by company ID, getting JSON back.
- Why it matters: faster, more reliable than retyping or screen automation.

Typical mistake: a technical definition the colleague would not understand.
:::

:::interview
How do you work with an external vendor on a delivery?
---
- Clear specification and acceptance criteria as the shared basis.
- Regular check-ins, questions log, documented decisions.
- Testing and UAT, defect triage, defect vs change request.
- Status reporting and escalation when needed; respectful but firm.

Typical mistake: treating the vendor as an opponent, or accepting deliveries without checking them.
:::
